\subsection{Pre-Internship Monitoring Posture}

Before my internship began in mid-March~2026, the IA-SRE team operated
without a centralized observability stack. Monitoring for production
financial automation workloads relied entirely on an \emph{ad~hoc} model:
business teams in Dublin, London, and Halifax would notice anomalies in
their fund processing outputs and raise tickets. There were no CloudWatch
alarms on Meridian's Apache Kafka (MSK) consumer lag, no dashboards
aggregating ECS task health across the five platforms, no
SLO tracking for message-processing throughput, and no mechanism for the
SRE team to receive proactive notification of degrading conditions. The
team was, in the language of the four golden signals introduced later by
mentor Kri Labos, entirely blind to \emph{saturation} and
\emph{error rate} signals until they had already caused downstream
business impact.

This posture was not unusual for the stage of the platform's maturity.
The IA team had grown rapidly, and engineering effort had been focused on
building automation capabilities rather than instrumenting them. By early
2026, however, the breadth of production workloads --- 119 Lambda
functions, 22 ECS clusters, critical Kafka-backed event pipelines across
five platforms --- had reached a scale at which reactive
monitoring was no longer adequate. The April~17 incident made that
inadequacy undeniable.

\subsection{The April 17, 2026 Meridian Production Incident}

On \textbf{April~17, 2026}, the Meridian platform experienced a
\textbf{7.5-hour processing delay} that halted daily statement processing
for BPM Outsource Cases affecting multiple fund clients, including
Winton, Goldman Sachs dailies, and others.

The root cause was an unexpected surge of automated system events from
Citco Recs. These events flooded the OpenSearch consumption layer,
which processes and indexes Kafka events into the OpenSearch cluster that
AXI queries for BPM completion status. Events were being received
successfully in Databricks but were not being consumed into OpenSearch,
causing the Kafka consumer lag to balloon to approximately
\textbf{9~million messages} queued on the affected topics. Because AXI
polls Meridian for completion events rather than receiving push
notifications, the backed-up consumer meant AXI saw no completions and
stalled.

The incident timeline (Table~\ref{tab:apr17_timeline_ch4}) illustrates
the observability gap in concrete terms. BPM submitted its first
completion event at 04:39~AST. AXI polled nine times over the next two
hours with no result. The SRE team received no alert. The issue surfaced
only when the \textbf{Dublin business team escalated it at 08:13} ---
three and a half hours after the first failure signal and more than
ninety minutes after AXI had exhausted its retry budget. Resolution
required scaling the OpenSearch consumer concurrency from 30 to 60
partitions, upgrading the OpenSearch cluster from a
\texttt{t3.medium.search} to \texttt{m4.large.search} with 5~nodes
(up from 3), and upscaling both the Interactions Service API and the
Tasks Management API memory allocations. By approximately 12:00~AST,
normal processing had resumed --- but not before causing a half-day delay
for fund clients with time-sensitive NAV and statement delivery windows.

\subsection{From Incident to Programme}

The April~17 post-mortem identified two systemic deficiencies that
became the engineering mandate for the internship:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{No proactive monitoring on Kafka consumer lag.} Had a
          CloudWatch alarm been set on the MSK \texttt{EstimatedMaxTimeLag}
          metric, the SRE team would have received notification within
          minutes of the backlog crossing a safe threshold --- before AXI
          had polled even once. Post-deployment, the lag detection latency
          improved from the observed 2--3~minute manual discovery window to
          under \textbf{30~seconds} via automated alarm.

    \item \textbf{No centralized visibility across the IA platform.} The
          incident's blast radius --- Winton, Goldman Sachs, and other
          fund clients --- was a direct consequence of having no single
          pane of glass from which to observe the health of the Meridian
          event pipeline alongside the SQS queues, ECS task counts, Lambda
          error rates, and OpenSearch indexing throughput that together
          determined end-to-end processing status.
\end{enumerate}

These two gaps directly motivated the three parallel work streams
described in the following section. The incident served as both a
technical catalyst and an organizational forcing function: Kishor
elevated IISS-487 to \textbf{Critical} priority on May~6, 2026, and
my internship pivoted from exploratory dashboard research to a
full-scale observability platform build.

\section{Work Streams}
\label{sec:work_streams}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Three parallel work streams organized my internship's engineering scope.
Each stream addressed a distinct layer of the observability and
automation platform, but all three shared the same underlying motivation:
making the health of the IA platform continuously visible, queryable, and
actionable.

\subsection{Work Stream 1: SRE Observability Platform (IISS-487)}

The primary deliverable of my internship was a \textbf{centralized
Grafana Amazon Managed Grafana (AMG) dashboard platform} providing
single-pane visibility across all IA production workloads.

The observability platform was built on four architectural layers:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Data layer.} Amazon CloudWatch serves as the sole
          data source, surfacing native AWS service metrics via the
          Grafana CloudWatch plugin. Metric Insights SQL queries
          (\texttt{SELECT} with \texttt{GROUP BY LABELS}) enable
          per-resource drill-down across Lambda functions, ECS tasks,
          MSK consumer groups, and SQS queues.

    \item \textbf{Visualization layer.} The Grafana workspace hosts
          \textbf{14~dashboard rows} covering seven monitored platforms
          (Lambda, ECS, MSK/Kafka, SQS, DocumentDB, OpenSearch, EC2)
          plus cross-service health, performance, alerting, and a
          resource inventory overview. In total, the dashboard aggregates
          \textbf{110+~panels}, including time-series graphs, stat panels,
          bar gauges, heatmaps, and resource tables with deep-link URLs
          into the AWS Console.

    \item \textbf{Alerting layer.} \textbf{38~CloudWatch alarms} were
          defined across all seven platforms using a parameterized
          CloudFormation YAML template. Each alarm is wired to an SNS
          topic that routes notifications to the SRE team's email
          distribution list. Composite alarms aggregate per-service
          conditions into platform-level health signals.

    \item \textbf{Infrastructure-as-Code layer.} I developed a Lambda-backed
          \textbf{CloudFormation IaC provisioner} to
          enable dynamic alarm deployment at scale. This addressed the
          constraint that the Secrets Manager parameter store has a 64KB
          payload limit --- when the agentic CloudFormation provisioner
          grew beyond this limit (at 14,720~lines of generated YAML), the
          architecture was refactored to use an S3 bucket as the template
          staging location, with the Lambda function fetching and deploying
          stacks from S3 at runtime.
\end{itemize}

Infrastructure was deployed across four CloudFormation stacks on
\textbf{May~11, 2026}, and I enabled Container Insights on five of the
six Meridian ECS clusters on May~11--12. The Grafana workspace underwent
a significant version upgrade from \textbf{10.4 to 12.4} on
\textbf{May~13}, which required migrating all panel SQL queries from
legacy CloudWatch Metrics Insights syntax to the updated query model and
resolving breaking changes in visualization configuration.

The total effort I logged against IISS-487 reached \textbf{140~hours} over
the period from May~6 to June~18, 2026.

\subsection{Work Stream 2: IA Infrastructure Monitoring (IISS-482 and Sub-Stories)}

    \item \textbf{IISS-482 SQS Feature}: A SQS monitoring feature was

\subsection{Work Stream 3: IA Resources Inventory API (IISS-507)}

The third work stream produced a \textbf{Flask REST API} that I built for
programmatic discovery of all IA platform cloud resources. The inventory
API --- implemented in the \texttt{ia-sre-resources-inventory} repository
--- provides endpoints for querying Lambda functions, ECS clusters, EC2
instances, Secrets Manager secrets, and S3 buckets across all five
platforms.

The core architectural pattern is a \textbf{discoverer registry}: a
registry of concrete discoverer classes, each responsible for a specific
resource type and platform combination. This pattern enables new
budget codes and resource types to be added without modifying the core
API layer. At the time of writing, 3~concrete discoverers and 7~stub
discoverers were implemented, with the full registry covering:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{119~Lambda functions} across 5~platforms
          (\texttt{rpacfs1}, \texttt{ia-bpo}, \texttt{automationhub},
          \texttt{meridian}, \texttt{citcoworks})
    \item \textbf{22~ECS clusters} across 5~platforms (with a
          critical discovery challenge: \texttt{CITCOWORKS} and
          \texttt{MERIDIAN} share the \texttt{``Shared Cloud Artifacts''}
          BudgetCode tag, requiring \texttt{AppName} as a mandatory
          secondary key for disambiguation)
    \item \textbf{83~EC2 instances} scanned with tag-based discovery
          using the \texttt{BudgetCode} primary tag and
          \texttt{AppName} fallback
\end{itemize}

I developed the API with a strong emphasis on test coverage: the
codebase includes \textbf{562~automated tests} achieving
\textbf{99\%~code coverage}. The test suite covers unit tests for each
discoverer, integration tests for the REST endpoints, property-based
tests for the tag-matching logic, and edge case tests for malformed
resource tags and empty platforms.

\subsection{IISS-507 and the Inventory API}

IISS-507 (\texttt{ia-sre-resources-inventory}) was scaffolded on
June~11, 2026, as a new Flask async API project separate from the
existing monitoring repositories. My key architectural decision was the
\textbf{discoverer registry pattern}: rather than hardcoding resource
discovery logic per budget code, a registry maps
\texttt{(resource\_type, budget\_code)} tuples to concrete discoverer
classes. This design enables the team to onboard new budget codes or
resource types by implementing a single discoverer class and registering
it --- no changes to routing, serialization, or test infrastructure.

The tag-based discovery challenge was non-trivial. Two platforms --- \texttt{CITCOWORKS} and \texttt{MERIDIAN} --- share the
AWS tag value \texttt{``Shared Cloud Artifacts''} for the
\texttt{BudgetCode} dimension. A naive \texttt{BudgetCode} lookup would
therefore conflate resources from two distinct operational environments.
The solution was to designate the \texttt{AppName} tag as a mandatory
secondary discriminator for these two environments, with the discovery
logic performing a two-stage filter: \texttt{BudgetCode=``Shared Cloud
Artifacts''} followed by \texttt{AppName} $\in$ \{\texttt{citcoworks}, \texttt{meridian}\}.

\subsection{Support Incidents and Hotfixes}

Four RPA support rotations (Weeks~8, 12, 16, and~20) and several
out-of-rotation incidents rounded out my project scope:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{IISS-706 (June~8, 7h)}: The CAIS Deadline process
          failed with a \texttt{SpnegoError} caused by empty UCM
          credentials. My root cause analysis revealed that the ECS
          container loaded credentials from Secrets Manager once at
          module import time via \texttt{constants.py}. When the UCM
          secret was rotated or expired between container restarts, the
          cached empty credential persisted for the entire container
          lifecycle. I implemented a fail-fast validation layer
          and a second credential check before the UCM upload step,
          making the failure visible immediately rather than manifesting
          as a cryptic downstream NTLM authentication error.

    \item \textbf{IISS-741 (July~1, Critical)}: MESO month-end tasks
          became stuck for the INNOCAP and SAMLMOS funds, requiring
          manual retrigger of the \texttt{ia\_meso\_process} pipeline.

    \item \textbf{IISS-753 (July~8)}: VPL IPV process encountered a UI
          selector failure, with a 16-hour resolution estimate involving
          selector resilience analysis across the UiPath automation.

    \item \textbf{IISS-876 (July~30)}: VPL Pricing GTL incorrect booking
          required root cause analysis and a targeted process fix.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Meridian Platform}
\label{sec:meridian_platform}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\textbf{Meridian} is Citco's core digital workflow platform for fund
operations. It handles event processing, task management, and document
workflows for fund administration --- serving as the operational backbone
through which business events (fund completions, report deliveries, task
assignments) flow between Citco's systems and external counterparties.
Understanding Meridian's architecture was prerequisite to my designing
meaningful observability coverage for it.

\subsection{Meridian Components Relevant to Observability}

The following Meridian components were my primary observability targets
during the internship:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Event Routing Service (ERS).} Routes events between
          services using a mapping table that specifies area, subject,
          source, type, and destination Action Processor. Events are stored
          in an ODL/Redis Elasticache layer and then pushed to Kafka topics
          for consumption. ERS cannot perform a pass-through without a
          mapping entry; missing \texttt{advanced\_matching} fields cause
          routing failures.

    \item \textbf{Action Processor (AP).} Consumes routed events from Kafka
          and executes the corresponding business logic (e.g., updating task
          status, triggering downstream notifications). APs are registered
          via \texttt{deployment\_specs.yaml} using the PyTOML library and
          can be anything from a simple status update to a cross-system
          integration call.

    \item \textbf{OpenSearch Consumer.} Consumes events from Kafka topics
          and indexes them into the OpenSearch search cluster, making events
          queryable by downstream services such as AXI. This is the
          component whose throughput was overwhelmed on April~17, 2026 ---
          the incident that directly motivated the SRE observability work.

    \item \textbf{Apache Kafka / Amazon MSK.} The message backbone of the
          Meridian event architecture. Kafka topics serve as the durable
          buffer between event producers (ERS) and consumers (OpenSearch
          consumer, APs). Consumer lag on these topics is the primary
          leading indicator of event processing delays.

    \item \textbf{ECS Clusters (Meridian).} Six ECS clusters host the
          containerized Meridian services:
          \texttt{meridian-api-cluster},
          \texttt{meridian-cluster-compute},
          \texttt{meridian-cluster-services},
          \texttt{meridian-services-cluster},
          \texttt{meridian-ui-cluster}, and
          \texttt{meridian-compute-cluster}.
          I enabled Container Insights on five of these six clusters on
          May~11--12, 2026 (IISS-487), providing CPU, memory, network I/O,
          and storage utilization telemetry at the task level.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The April 17, 2026 Incident}

On \textbf{April~17, 2026}, event processing on the Meridian platform
experienced a 7.5-hour delay that prevented AXI from receiving BPM
completion events for daily statement processing. The root cause was a
high volume of automated system events from Citco Recs, which overwhelmed
the OpenSearch consumption layer. Events were received successfully in
Databricks but were not being consumed into OpenSearch --- backing up
approximately \textbf{9~million messages} on the Kafka topics. Multiple
funds were affected, including Winton, Goldman Sachs dailies, and others.

The timeline illustrates the core observability gap: BPM submitted the
first completion event at 04:39 AST; AXI polled nine times over the
following two hours with no response; and the issue was not flagged until
the \textbf{Dublin business team raised it at 08:13} --- over three and a
half hours after the first failure signal.

The resolution involved doubling the concurrency and Kafka partition counts
for the OpenSearch consumer (30$\to$60 concurrent consumers, 30$\to$60
partitions), upgrading the OpenSearch cluster from a \texttt{t3.medium} to
\texttt{m4.large} with the node count increased from 3 to 5, and upscaling
the Interactions Service and Tasks Management API memory allocations.

The incident directly exposed a critical gap in the team's monitoring
posture: \textbf{no proactive monitoring or alerting was in place on the
SRE side for Meridian event volume anomalies.} The SRE team had no
visibility into Kafka consumer lag, OpenSearch ingestion rates, or ECS
task-level resource saturation. This gap became the primary motivation for:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{IISS-487} --- the centralized Grafana SRE dashboard,
          designed to surface Meridian consumer lag as a leading indicator;
    \item \textbf{IISS-489} --- the formal SRE Observability Roadmap, which
          mapped monitoring gaps to prioritized engineering actions;
    \item \textbf{IISS-488} --- the Auto-Healing Pipeline initiative, aimed
          at reducing the mean time to recovery for recurring failure modes;
    \item \textbf{IISS-506} --- the Meridian monitoring infrastructure
          project, providing a full gap analysis and remediation plan.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Earlier Observability Blind Spot (January--February 2026)}

The April~17 incident, while the most dramatic and well-documented failure,
was not the first signal of an observability gap at Citco. In the
\textbf{five-week period from mid-January to mid-February 2026}, a
production service was degraded or effectively down without any member of
the development, business, infrastructure, or DevOps teams realizing it.
The failure was eventually surfaced --- not by any automated alert or
monitoring system --- but by a client complaint that arrived weeks after the
underlying problem had begun affecting fund operations.

This five-week blind spot pre-dated the formal SRE engagement by several
months and had no documented root cause analysis because the problem had
persisted silently for so long that the precise onset was not recoverable
from logs. The incident became the organizational memory that gave weight to
Kishor's Dynatrace replacement initiative: it was not a hypothetical
reliability risk but a demonstrated pattern of extended, undetected service
degradation. The April~17 incident confirmed the pattern was still present
after the IA platform had grown to its current scale.

Together, these two incidents define the \textbf{observability mandate} that
governed my internship: build a system where a service degradation that
would have been invisible for five weeks in January~2026 would instead
trigger an alert within 30 seconds. The MSK consumer lag alarm I implemented --- which
would have fired within minutes of the April~17 backlog beginning to build
--- is the clearest demonstration that this mandate was met.

\section{Domain Immersion: Training Week (April 13--18, 2026)}

Before meaningful SRE or automation work could begin, I required

\subsection{RPA Support Processes}

The first two days (April~13--14) focused on the \textbf{Blue Prism and
UiPath} operational model. Key concepts covered:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{BPM architecture} --- the pool/queue model (resource pools,
          foreground vs.\ background execution, licensed-per-node
          multithreading), the PROD and UAT folder hierarchy
          (\texttt{COE $\to$ DataOps $\to$ Processes / Assets / Rules /
          Queues}), and the ongoing migration path from Blue Prism to
          UiPath Cloud.

    \item \textbf{UiPath architecture} --- the Orchestrator folder structure
          (CoE, FA~DataOps, etc.), trigger types (scheduled, API, SQS),
          runtime node heartbeat model, and the runtime $\to$ node
          $\to$ Orchestrator status-update loop.

    \item \textbf{Support escalation model} --- service desk tickets escalate
          to Horace, then to Kishor for management-level decisions. RPA
          Support rotations follow a weekly cadence with a defined on-call
          protocol for time-sensitive fund operations.

    \item \textbf{VPL queue monitoring} --- starting at 5~PM EST, checking
          queue depth, bot count, and completion status. Delays in VPL
          directly affect NAV delivery to clients and are therefore treated
          as high-severity incidents.

    \item \textbf{Corporate Actions template management} --- Mandatory,
          Voluntary, and Ops Transfer workflows are handled through a
          combination of Blue Prism processes and manual exception review.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{CitcoWorks Platform}

The April~14--15 sessions (delivered by Bhanu Teja Murari) covered the
\textbf{CitcoWorks} platform in detail:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Knowledge Graph (KG) hierarchy} --- a five-level hierarchy
          (L1: Product, L2: Process, L3: Sub-process, L4: Process Task,
          L5: Sub-task) maintained in a Neptune DB-backed graph and
          synchronized on-demand from the Optics source of truth. Each
          Friday, the team receives a workflow load email and updates the
          KG.

    \item \textbf{Event Routing Service and Action Processor} --- the ERS
          routes events (stored in ODL/Redis Elasticache, then Kafka) to
          registered APs based on the mapping table (Area, Subject, Source,
          Type). APs are deployed via \texttt{deployment\_specs.yaml} and
          implement business logic such as task status updates and
          cross-system integrations. The routing service cannot perform a
          pass-through; missing fields in advanced matching cause routing
          failures rather than silently dropped events.

    \item \textbf{AuthMaster} --- the Authentication and Authorization
          service governing user access to CitcoWorks. User personas are
          synced from AuthMaster into CitcoWorks at login, with a
          configurable TTL cache. Tenant roles determine which features and
          workflow views are accessible.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Meridian and Event Store}

Building directly on the CitcoWorks session, the April~15--16 sessions

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Event Store} --- Citco's structured logging library used
