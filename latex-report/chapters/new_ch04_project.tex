\section{Project Background and the Motivating Incident}
\label{sec:project_background}

\subsection{Pre-Internship Monitoring Posture}

Before my internship began in mid-Mar~2026, the IA-SRE team operated
without a centralized observability stack. Monitoring relied entirely on
an \emph{ad~hoc} model: business teams in Dublin, London, and Halifax
would notice anomalies in fund processing outputs and raise tickets. There
were no CloudWatch alarms, no dashboards aggregating health across the six
platforms, and no mechanism for the SRE team to receive proactive
notification of degrading conditions --- the team was blind to
\emph{saturation} and \emph{error rate} signals until they had already
caused downstream business impact.

\subsection{CAIS-Pricing Silent Outage (Jan--Feb 2026)}
\label{sec:cais_silent_outage}

Between \textbf{Jan~20 and Feb~17, 2026}, the CAIS-Pricing Extract GenAI
pipeline under the \texttt{rpacfs1} platform stopped delivering output for
\textbf{29 consecutive days} without a single alert firing. The root cause
was gradual exhaustion of the external CTI AI model API token, which
controls the async SQS callback that triggers the downstream Lambda
stages; without that callback, three downstream functions dropped to zero
invocations while two upstream functions kept running, making the pipeline
appear healthy. The outage was detected only when business users reported
missing pricing data --- 29~days after onset. The post-mortem (IISS-430)
identified the absence of throughput alarms, SQS queue depth monitoring,
and end-to-end output verification as the root causes of the failure going
unnoticed. Sub-task IISS-435 was assigned directly to me as the immediate
remediation and became the starting point for the full monitoring programme.

\subsection{The Meridian Incident (Apr~2026)}

On \textbf{Apr~17, 2026}, a surge of automated system events from Citco
Recs congested Meridian's OpenSearch consumption layer, causing Kafka
consumer lag to reach approximately \textbf{9~million messages}. Because
AXI polls Meridian for BPM completion events, the stalled consumer halted
daily statement processing for multiple fund clients --- including Winton
and Goldman Sachs --- for \textbf{7.5~hours}. The key timeline: BPM
submitted its first event at 04:39~AST; AXI polled nine times with no
result; the SRE team received no alert; the Dublin business team escalated
at 08:13~AST, three and a half hours after the first failure signal.
The post-mortem recorded explicitly: \textit{``No proactive
monitoring/alerting in place on the SRE side for Meridian event volume
anomalies.''} Action items from that post-mortem became the core
requirements for IISS-487.

\subsection{From Incident to Programme}

Both incidents shared the same root cause: no proactive monitoring. The
post-mortems established two specific gaps --- no Kafka consumer lag alarm,
and no centralized visibility across the IA-IT-SRE Infrastructure --- that
directly motivated the three work streams described in the following
section. Kishor elevated IISS-487 to \textbf{Critical} priority on
May~6, 2026, and my internship pivoted from exploratory dashboard research
to a full-scale observability platform build.

\section{Project Overview}
\label{sec:project_overview}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The two motivating incidents established a clear engineering mandate: build
proactive observability across the entire IA-IT-SRE Infrastructure. Three
work streams were pursued sequentially and in parallel, each one directly
motivated by the findings and limitations of the previous. A fourth
ongoing responsibility --- RPA production support --- ran throughout the
internship period.

\subsection{Work Stream 1: CloudWatch Dashboards and IA Infrastructure Monitoring}

Following the CAIS-Pricing Silent Outage, the immediate
remediation was to build a unified CloudWatch monitoring dashboard starting
with Lambda --- the AWS service at the centre of that incident. The goal was
to surface invocation count, error rate, duration, and throttle metrics
for every Lambda function across all six platforms, giving the SRE team
visibility that had not existed before.

Once Lambda was covered, the scope was extended incrementally to the other
AWS services in the IA-IT-SRE Infrastructure: SQS queues (queue depth and
oldest message age), EC2 instances (CPU and memory via CloudWatch Agent),
ECS clusters and tasks (via CloudWatch Container Insights), EKS node metrics,
and database services. Each resource type was added as a separate CloudWatch
dashboard, establishing the tag-based resource discovery patterns ---
including the \texttt{AppName} fallback strategy for shared \texttt{BudgetCode}
environments --- that all later work built upon.

These CloudWatch dashboards were effective for per-service visibility, but
they exposed a fundamental limitation: CloudWatch dashboards are siloed
per AWS service and do not support dynamic cross-service filtering. There was
no way to view Lambda, SQS, and ECS health for a single platform side by side
in one workspace. This limitation directly motivated Work Stream~2.
Key deliverables: IISS-435, IISS-455, IISS-461, IISS-469, IISS-482--486.

\subsection{Work Stream 2: Grafana AMG Centralized Dashboard}

Following the Meridian Incident, Kishor elevated IISS-487 to
\textbf{Critical} priority. The goal was to build a proof-of-concept (PoC)
centralized observability platform using \textbf{Amazon Managed Grafana
(AMG)} --- addressing what the per-service CloudWatch dashboards could not:
a unified, cross-platform, cross-service view of the entire IA-IT-SRE
Infrastructure in a single workspace.

The scope was defined by the Meridian post-mortem action items and refined
through requirements sessions with Kishor: the dashboard needed to cover
every AWS service already monitored in Work Stream~1 (Lambda, SQS, MSK/Kafka,
EC2, ECS, EKS, ECR, RDS Aurora, DocumentDB, Redis/Valkey, Neptune DB, OpenSearch),
support dynamic filtering by platform and AWS service via template variables,
and include CloudWatch alarms with SNS notifications to the SRE team. The
broader research objective was to evaluate whether this AWS-native stack could
achieve Dynatrace-equivalent observability --- documenting the feature parity
achieved, the gaps that remain, the limitations encountered, and the
incremental cost at each tier.

The dashboard was built on CloudWatch as the sole data source (via the native
Grafana CloudWatch plugin), using Metric Insights SQL for cross-resource
aggregation and CloudWatch Logs Insights for log-derived panels. An
Infrastructure-as-Code provisioner --- a Lambda-backed CloudFormation pipeline
--- was developed to dynamically discover the AWS service fleet at deploy time
and synthesize per-service alarm definitions, enabling a single deployment to
cover all monitored AWS services without manual enumeration. Key limitations
discovered and documented during this work stream --- including the Metric
Insights SQL percentile restriction, the SEARCH() visibility gap for idle
resources, and the 64~KB Secrets Manager payload limit --- are detailed in
Chapter~\ref{chap:methodologies}. Key deliverable: IISS-487 (140 hours).

\subsection{Work Stream 3: IA Infrastructure Inventory API}

A critical limitation surfaced during the Grafana dashboard build: the
\texttt{WHERE} clause in CloudWatch Metric Insights SQL does not support
pattern-based filtering (no wildcard or regex on dimension values). To filter
a panel to a specific platform, an exact dimension value match was required ---
but there was no reliable way to enumerate the available values dynamically from
within Grafana. The \texttt{dimension\_values()} function returned partial
results for large fleets, and cross-platform queries required hardcoding platform
names into template variable queries.

To solve this, the Lambda discovery script that had been built for the
CloudWatch dashboards was extended into a full-fledged \textbf{Flask REST API}:
the \texttt{ia-it-sre-infrastructure-inventory} service. The API discovers AWS
services across all six platforms, regions, and environments in real time at
query time, and exposes structured resource lists via REST endpoints. A Grafana
dashboard panel can call the API to retrieve the current list of Lambda
functions, ECS clusters, or SQS queues for a given platform, and use the
response to populate template variable options dynamically --- ensuring the
dashboard always reflects the live fleet without requiring manual updates.

The API uses a discoverer registry pattern: a registry maps
\texttt{(resource\_type, platform)} tuples to concrete discoverer classes,
allowing new platforms or AWS service types to be onboarded by implementing
a single class. The service was built with Python and \texttt{flask[async]},
using \texttt{boto3} for all AWS API calls and Pydantic for response validation.
The test suite achieved 99\% code coverage across unit, integration, and
property-based tests. Key deliverable: IISS-507.

\section{Project Goals}
\label{sec:project_goals}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The primary goals of the internship project were as follows:

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Eliminate reactive-only incident detection.} Replace the
          dependency on business-team escalation as the primary detection
          mechanism with proactive CloudWatch alarms that fire within seconds
          of a threshold being crossed, directly addressing the failure modes
          exposed by the CAIS-Pricing Silent Outage and the Meridian Incident.

    \item \textbf{Build a centralized observability platform for the IA-IT-SRE
          Infrastructure.} Provide a eagle-eye view across all six
          platforms and all 13 AWS service categories, enabling the SRE team
          to assess platform health, drill down to individual AWS services, and
          respond to incidents without navigating six separate CloudWatch consoles.

    \item \textbf{Automate AWS service discovery and inventory.} Replace manual
          Confluence-maintained Lambda lists with a programmatic, always-current
          inventory API that enumerates all deployed AWS services per platform
          and exposes them via REST endpoints for use by the dashboard and other
          downstream tooling.

    \item \textbf{Evaluate AWS-native observability as a Dynatrace alternative.}
          Conduct a structured feature-parity analysis comparing the AWS-native
          stack (CloudWatch, Grafana AMG, X-Ray, Application Signals) against
          Dynatrace capabilities, quantifying the achievable parity percentage
          and the incremental monthly cost at each tier.

    \item \textbf{Deliver Infrastructure-as-Code for all monitoring components.}
          Ensure all alarms, dashboards, IAM roles, and supporting infrastructure
          are provisioned via parameterized CloudFormation templates and AWS SAM,
          making the platform reproducible, version-controlled, and
          environment-portable.

    \item \textbf{Establish engineering quality standards.} Produce a test suite
          for the inventory API with 99\% code coverage, document all
          architectural decisions and limitations, and maintain a Confluence
          knowledge base for the Lambda inventory, runbooks, and incident
          post-mortems.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Centralized Grafana SRE Dashboard (IISS-487)}

\textbf{Platform:} Amazon Managed Grafana (AMG) v12.4, CloudWatch as
sole data source via the native Grafana CloudWatch plugin.

\textbf{Data layer:} Amazon CloudWatch Metrics and CloudWatch Logs
Insights. Metric Insights SQL (\texttt{SELECT ... FROM namespace GROUP BY
dimension}) used for all multi-resource panels --- enables a single query
to return per-resource time series across the full fleet without one panel
per resource. CloudWatch Logs Insights used for log-derived error panels
with structured JSON field extraction.

\textbf{Dashboard structure:} 14 collapsible rows, one per AWS service
category --- Lambda, SQS, MSK/Kafka, EC2, ECR, EKS, ECS Services, ECS
Target Groups, RDS Aurora, DocumentDB, Redis/Valkey, Neptune DB, and
OpenSearch. Cross-service filtering is implemented via Grafana template
variables backed by CloudWatch \texttt{dimension\_values()} queries,
allowing operators to scope the entire workspace to a single platform or
AWS service via dropdown selection.

\textbf{Alerting layer:} CloudWatch alarms provisioned across all
monitored AWS services, each wired to an SNS topic routing to the SRE
team email distribution list (\texttt{ia\_it\_sre@citco.com}). Composite
alarms aggregate per-service states into platform-level health signals.

\textbf{Infrastructure-as-Code (IaC):} AWS SAM + parameterized
CloudFormation YAML templates (one template per AWS service category).
A Lambda-backed CloudFormation custom resource provisioner dynamically
discovers the current fleet at deploy time and synthesizes alarm
\texttt{AWS::CloudWatch::Alarm} resource definitions, deployed across
four stacks: Lambda alarms, ECS alarms, SQS alarms, and MSK alarms.
Templates are staged in S3 (migrated from Secrets Manager due to the
64~KB payload limit) and fetched by the provisioner Lambda at runtime.

\textbf{IAM model:} 16-role cross-account agent permission model granting
the Grafana service account read-only access to CloudWatch metrics, logs,
and EC2 tags across all IA AWS accounts. Each role is scoped to
\texttt{cloudwatch:GetMetricData}, \texttt{cloudwatch:ListMetrics},
\texttt{logs:StartQuery}, \texttt{logs:GetQueryResults}, and
\texttt{ec2:DescribeTags}.

\textbf{Container Insights:} Enabled on five of the six Meridian ECS
clusters, publishing task-level CPU, memory, network I/O, and storage
telemetry into the \texttt{ECS/ContainerInsights} namespace, surfaced in
the ECS dashboard rows.

\subsection{IA Infrastructure Monitoring Platform (IISS-482--486)}

\textbf{Platform:} Native Amazon CloudWatch dashboards built via
\texttt{aws\_cw\_dashboard} CloudFormation resources and Python-based
dashboard builders.

\textbf{Scope:} Per-platform CloudWatch dashboards covering Lambda
(invocations, errors, duration, throttles, concurrent executions), EC2
(CPU, memory via CloudWatch Agent, disk I/O, network), ECS (service CPU
and memory via Container Insights), EKS (node and pod metrics), and SQS
(queue depth, oldest message age). Each dashboard supports tag-based
resource filtering using \texttt{BudgetCode} as the primary tag and
\texttt{AppName} as the mandatory fallback discriminator for
\texttt{meridian} and \texttt{citcoworks} platforms.

\textbf{SQS Registry Architecture (IISS-482):} A registry-based SQS
monitoring pipeline with a three-tier fallback model: (1)~live
CloudWatch API query; (2)~S3-cached snapshot on API failure; (3)~locally
bundled queue inventory as the final fallback. This ensures the SQS
monitoring pipeline remains functional during AWS service degradation
events.

\textbf{Key limitation driving WS3:} CloudWatch Metric Insights SQL
\texttt{WHERE} clause does not support pattern matching or regex on
dimension values. Enumerating resource names dynamically for Grafana
template variables required a dedicated external API rather than relying
on \texttt{dimension\_values()} alone.

\subsection{IA Infrastructure Inventory API (IISS-507)}

\textbf{Stack:} Python~3, \texttt{flask[async]}, \texttt{boto3},
Pydantic, \texttt{serverless-wsgi} (Lambda deployment adapter),
\texttt{pytest} + Hypothesis (property-based testing).

\textbf{Architecture --- Discoverer Registry Pattern:} A central registry
maps \texttt{(resource\_type, platform)} tuples to concrete discoverer
classes, each implementing \texttt{async def discover() -> List[Resource]}.
New platforms or AWS service types are onboarded by implementing a single
class --- zero changes to routing, serialization, or test infrastructure.
Route handlers use \texttt{asyncio.gather()} to dispatch all relevant
discoverers concurrently, keeping end-to-end API latency within the
10-second NFR target across the full fleet.

\textbf{Tag disambiguation:} The \texttt{meridian} and \texttt{citcoworks}
platforms share the \texttt{BudgetCode=``Shared Cloud Artifacts''} tag.
A mandatory two-stage filter --- stage~1 \texttt{BudgetCode} match,
stage~2 \texttt{AppName} discriminator --- separates resources from the
two platforms and is enforced in all discoverers for those environments.

\textbf{API endpoints:} \texttt{GET /api/v1/lambda},
\texttt{/api/v1/ecs}, \texttt{/api/v1/ec2}, \texttt{/api/v1/sqs},
\texttt{/api/v1/s3}, \texttt{/api/v1/health}. All endpoints support an
optional \texttt{?platform=} query parameter for filtered discovery.

\textbf{Pydantic response model:} Each discovered resource carries five
fields: \texttt{name}, \texttt{type}, \texttt{platform},
\texttt{tags} (free-form dict), \texttt{arn}.

\textbf{Test suite:} 99\% code coverage across unit tests (per-discoverer
with mocked \texttt{boto3}), integration tests (full Flask test client),
property-based tests via Hypothesis (tag-matching logic), and edge-case
tests (malformed tags, empty platforms, pagination edge cases).

\section{The Meridian Platform}
\label{sec:meridian_platform}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\textbf{Meridian} is Citco's core digital workflow platform for fund
operations. It handles event processing, task management, and document
workflows for fund administration --- serving as the operational backbone
through which business events (fund completions, report deliveries, task
assignments) flow between Citco's systems and external counterparties.
Understanding Meridian's architecture was prerequisite to my designing
meaningful observability coverage for it.

\subsection{Meridian Components Relevant to Observability}

The following Meridian components were my primary observability targets
during the internship:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Event Routing Service (ERS).} Routes events between
          services using a mapping table that specifies area, subject,
          source, type, and destination Action Processor. Events are stored
          in an ODL/Redis Elasticache layer and then pushed to Kafka topics
          for consumption. ERS cannot perform a pass-through without a
          mapping entry; missing \texttt{advanced\_matching} fields cause
          routing failures.

    \item \textbf{Action Processor (AP).} Consumes routed events from Kafka
          and executes the corresponding business logic (e.g., updating task
          status, triggering downstream notifications). APs are registered
          via \texttt{deployment\_specs.yaml} using the PyTOML library and
          can be anything from a simple status update to a cross-system
          integration call.

    \item \textbf{OpenSearch Consumer.} Consumes events from Kafka topics
          and indexes them into the OpenSearch search cluster, making events
          queryable by downstream services such as AXI. This is the
          component whose throughput was overwhelmed on Apr~17, 2026 ---
          the incident that directly motivated the SRE observability work.

    \item \textbf{Apache Kafka / Amazon MSK.} The message backbone of the
          Meridian event architecture. Kafka topics serve as the durable
          buffer between event producers (ERS) and consumers (OpenSearch
          consumer, APs). Consumer lag on these topics is the primary
          leading indicator of event processing delays.

    \item \textbf{ECS Clusters (Meridian).} Six ECS clusters host the
          containerized Meridian services:
          \texttt{meridian-api-cluster},
          \texttt{meridian-cluster-compute},
          \texttt{meridian-cluster-services},
          \texttt{meridian-services-cluster},
          \texttt{meridian-ui-cluster}, and
          \texttt{meridian-compute-cluster}.
          I enabled Container Insights on five of these six clusters on
          May~11--12, 2026 (IISS-487), providing CPU, memory, network I/O,
          and storage utilization telemetry at the task level.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Meridian Incident}

On \textbf{Apr~17, 2026}, event processing on the Meridian platform
experienced a 7.5-hour delay that prevented AXI from receiving BPM
completion events for daily statement processing. The root cause was a
high volume of automated system events from Citco Recs, which overwhelmed
the OpenSearch consumption layer. Events were received successfully in
Databricks but were not being consumed into OpenSearch --- backing up
approximately \textbf{9~million messages} on the Kafka topics. Multiple
funds were affected, including Winton, Goldman Sachs dailies, and others.

The timeline illustrates the core observability gap: BPM submitted the
first completion event at 04:39 AST; AXI polled nine times over the
following two hours with no response; and the issue was not flagged until
the \textbf{Dublin business team raised it at 08:13} --- over three and a
half hours after the first failure signal.

The resolution involved doubling the concurrency and Kafka partition counts
for the OpenSearch consumer (30$\to$60 concurrent consumers, 30$\to$60
partitions), upgrading the OpenSearch cluster from a \texttt{t3.medium} to
\texttt{m4.large} with the node count increased from 3 to 5, and upscaling
the Interactions Service and Tasks Management API memory allocations.

The incident directly exposed a critical gap in the team's monitoring
posture: \textbf{no proactive monitoring or alerting was in place on the
SRE side for Meridian event volume anomalies.} The SRE team had no
visibility into Kafka consumer lag, OpenSearch ingestion rates, or ECS
task-level resource saturation. This gap became the primary motivation for:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{IISS-487} --- the centralized Grafana SRE dashboard,
          designed to surface Meridian consumer lag as a leading indicator;
    \item \textbf{IISS-489} --- the formal SRE Observability Roadmap, which
          mapped monitoring gaps to prioritized engineering actions;
    \item \textbf{IISS-488} --- the Auto-Healing Pipeline initiative, aimed
          at reducing the mean time to recovery for recurring failure modes;
    \item \textbf{IISS-506} --- the Meridian monitoring infrastructure
          project, providing a full gap analysis and remediation plan.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Earlier Observability Blind Spot (Jan--Feb 2026)}

The Apr~17 incident, while the most dramatic and well-documented failure,
was not the first signal of an observability gap at Citco. In the
\textbf{five-week period from mid-Jan to mid-Feb 2026}, a
production service was degraded or effectively down without any member of
the development, business, infrastructure, or DevOps teams realizing it.
The failure was eventually surfaced --- not by any automated alert or
monitoring system --- but by a client complaint that arrived weeks after the
underlying problem had begun affecting fund operations.

This five-week blind spot pre-dated the formal SRE engagement by several
months and had no documented root cause analysis because the problem had
persisted silently for so long that the precise onset was not recoverable
from logs. The incident became the organizational memory that gave weight to
Kishor's Dynatrace replacement initiative: it was not a hypothetical
reliability risk but a demonstrated pattern of extended, undetected service
degradation. The Apr~17 incident confirmed the pattern was still present
after the IA platform had grown to its current scale.

Together, these two incidents define the \textbf{observability mandate} that
governed my internship: build a system where a service degradation that
would have been invisible for five weeks in Jan~2026 would instead
trigger an alert within 30 seconds. The MSK consumer lag alarm I implemented --- which
would have fired within minutes of the Apr~17 backlog beginning to build
--- is the clearest demonstration that this mandate was met.

\subsection{RPA Platforms and Key Business Processes}

The IA platform's automation workloads operate on two RPA technologies:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blue Prism} handles daily, monthly, and quarterly
          fund operations automation. Blue Prism is deployed in pools of
          licensed robot nodes; each pool handles a designated class of
          work (e.g., DataOps P1--P20 in PROD).

    \item \textbf{UiPath} --- both UiPath Cloud (most processes, post
          migration) and UiPath on-premises (non-cloud, for processes such
          as FX Settled Status audits that run within the Citco VPC) ---
          is the target platform for Blue Prism migrations and hosts newer
          automation processes.
\end{itemize}

The key business processes monitored by the IA-SRE team include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{VPL Pricing} --- Valuation and Pricing (VPL) queue
          monitored from 5~PM EST daily. Three primary bots (with capacity
          for three additional) handle fund NAV and pricing completion
          events under a tight business deadline driven by market close.

    \item \textbf{Corporate Actions} --- Mandatory, Voluntary, and
          Operations Transfer workflows, plus Two Sigma wire transfers.

    \item \textbf{MESO} --- Middle Office Month-End Sign-Off
          (\texttt{ia\_meso\_process}), a Python and AWS-based process
          handling cash and position reconciliation, Aexeo report
          generation, and E-Binder packaging for fund administration teams.

    \item \textbf{CAIS Deadline} --- Consolidated Audit Trail (CAIS)
          submission automation under the \texttt{ia-bpo} budget code,
          subject to regulatory filing deadlines.
\end{itemize}
