\section{The Citco Group of Companies}

The Citco Group of Companies (Citco) is the world's largest independent hedge fund administrator, with over \$1.8 trillion in assets under administration. Originally founded in 1948 as the \textit{Curaçao International Trust Company} in Curaçao, Netherlands Antilles, the firm is currently headquartered in Tortola, British Virgin Islands. Over more than seven decades, Citco has grown from a civil law notary practice serving private clients and multinationals into a global financial services conglomerate operating across 50+ offices worldwide with approximately 16,000 employees spanning North America, Europe, Asia-Pacific, and the Caribbean.

Citco remains majority independently owned, a distinction that underpins its client-centric operating philosophy. The firm's CEO, Christopher Smeets, leads a management team that has overseen sustained organic growth --- from \$250 billion in administered assets in 2005 to the current \$1.8 trillion benchmark. This growth trajectory reflects Citco's pioneering role in shaping the fund administration sector and its continuous investment in proprietary technology platforms to service increasingly complex alternative investment structures.

\subsection{Core Service Divisions}

Citco's global operations are organized across several specialized divisions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Citco Fund Services (CFS) \& Investor Relations} --- The flagship division and global leader in services for the alternative investment industry. CFS provides full-service fund administration, fund accounting, net asset value (NAV) calculations, investor relations, and reporting for over 2,000 funds. CFS has maintained SOC~1 Type~2 certification for 22 consecutive years and achieved its inaugural SOC~2 Type~2 certification in 2025, underscoring its commitment to operational controls and data security.

    \item \textbf{Citco Governance Services} --- Provides corporate and global subsidiary governance, secretarial services, financial accounting and reporting, tax and audit liaison, entity incorporation, and market entry services across multiple jurisdictions.

    \item \textbf{Citco Banking} --- Offers clients a comprehensive suite of banking services including investor accounts, operational and deposit bank accounts, foreign exchange, and credit services.

    \item \textbf{Citco Technology Management (CTM)} --- The internal technology arm responsible for developing and maintaining Citco's proprietary technology ecosystem, cloud infrastructure, data platforms, and enterprise applications that power fund administration and middle-office operations globally.

    \item \textbf{Middle Office Solutions} --- Encompassing Treasury and Collateral Management, OTC settlements, Trade Matching, and Portfolio Finance. Citco's cloud-based \AE xeo\textsuperscript{\textregistered} Treasury platform provides centralized wire approval and fund movement visibility.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Meridian Platform}
\label{sec:meridian_platform}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\textbf{Meridian} is Citco's core digital workflow platform for fund
operations. It handles event processing, task management, and document
workflows for fund administration --- serving as the operational backbone
through which business events (fund completions, report deliveries, task
assignments) flow between Citco's systems and external counterparties.
Understanding Meridian's architecture was prerequisite to my designing
meaningful observability coverage for it.

\subsection{Meridian Components Relevant to Observability}

The following Meridian components were my primary observability targets
during the internship:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Event Routing Service (ERS).} Routes events between
          services using a mapping table that specifies area, subject,
          source, type, and destination Action Processor. Events are stored
          in an ODL/Redis Elasticache layer and then pushed to Kafka topics
          for consumption. ERS cannot perform a pass-through without a
          mapping entry; missing \texttt{advanced\_matching} fields cause
          routing failures.

    \item \textbf{Action Processor (AP).} Consumes routed events from Kafka
          and executes the corresponding business logic (e.g., updating task
          status, triggering downstream notifications). APs are registered
          via \texttt{deployment\_specs.yaml} using the PyTOML library and
          can be anything from a simple status update to a cross-system
          integration call.

    \item \textbf{OpenSearch Consumer.} Consumes events from Kafka topics
          and indexes them into the OpenSearch search cluster, making events
          queryable by downstream services such as AXI. This is the
          component whose throughput was overwhelmed during the Meridian Incident ---
          the incident that directly motivated the SRE observability work.

    \item \textbf{Apache Kafka / Amazon MSK.} The message backbone of the
          Meridian event architecture. Kafka topics serve as the durable
          buffer between event producers (ERS) and consumers (OpenSearch
          consumer, APs). Consumer lag on these topics is the primary
          leading indicator of event processing delays.

    \item \textbf{ECS Clusters (Meridian).} Six ECS clusters host the
          containerized Meridian services:
          \texttt{meridian-api-cluster},
          \texttt{meridian-cluster-compute},
          \texttt{meridian-cluster-services},
          \texttt{meridian-services-cluster},
          \texttt{meridian-ui-cluster}, and
          \texttt{meridian-compute-cluster}.
          I enabled Container Insights on five of these six clusters on
          May~11--12, 2026 (IISS-487), providing CPU, memory, network I/O,
          and storage utilization telemetry at the task level.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Meridian Incident}

On \textbf{Apr~17, 2026}, event processing on the Meridian platform
experienced a 7.5-hour delay that prevented AXI from receiving BPM
completion events for daily statement processing. The root cause was a
high volume of automated system events from Citco Recs, which overwhelmed
the OpenSearch consumption layer. Events were received successfully in
Databricks but were not being consumed into OpenSearch --- backing up
approximately \textbf{9~million messages} on the Kafka topics. Multiple
funds were affected, including Winton, Goldman Sachs dailies, and others.

The timeline illustrates the core observability gap: BPM submitted the
first completion event at 04:39 AST; AXI polled nine times over the
following two hours with no response; and the issue was not flagged until
the \textbf{Dublin business team raised it at 08:13} --- over three and a
half hours after the first failure signal.

The resolution involved doubling the concurrency and Kafka partition counts
for the OpenSearch consumer (30$\to$60 concurrent consumers, 30$\to$60
partitions), upgrading the OpenSearch cluster from a \texttt{t3.medium} to
\texttt{m4.large} with the node count increased from 3 to 5, and upscaling
the Interactions Service and Tasks Management API memory allocations.

The incident directly exposed a critical gap in the team's monitoring
posture: \textbf{no proactive monitoring or alerting was in place on the
SRE side for Meridian event volume anomalies.} The SRE team had no
visibility into Kafka consumer lag, OpenSearch ingestion rates, or ECS
task-level resource saturation. This gap became the primary motivation for:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{IISS-487} --- the centralized Grafana SRE dashboard,
          designed to surface Meridian consumer lag as a leading indicator;
    \item \textbf{IISS-489} --- the formal SRE Observability Roadmap, which
          mapped monitoring gaps to prioritized engineering actions;
    \item \textbf{IISS-488} --- the Auto-Healing Pipeline initiative, aimed
          at reducing the mean time to recovery for recurring failure modes;
    \item \textbf{IISS-506} --- the Meridian monitoring infrastructure
          project, providing a full gap analysis and remediation plan.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Earlier Observability Blind Spot (Jan--Feb 2026)}

The Meridian Incident, while the most dramatic and well-documented failure,
was not the first signal of an observability gap at Citco. In the
\textbf{five-week period from mid-Jan to mid-Feb 2026}, a
production service was degraded or effectively down without any member of
the development, business, infrastructure, or DevOps teams realizing it.
The failure was eventually surfaced --- not by any automated alert or
monitoring system --- but by a client complaint that arrived weeks after the
underlying problem had begun affecting fund operations.

This five-week blind spot pre-dated the formal SRE engagement by several
months and had no documented root cause analysis because the problem had
persisted silently for so long that the precise onset was not recoverable
from logs. The incident became the organizational memory that gave weight to
Kishor's Dynatrace replacement initiative: it was not a hypothetical
reliability risk but a demonstrated pattern of extended, undetected service
degradation. The Meridian Incident confirmed the pattern was still present
after the IA platform had grown to its current scale.

Together, these two incidents define the \textbf{observability mandate} that
governed my internship: build a system where a service degradation that
would have been invisible for five weeks in Jan~2026 would instead
trigger an alert within 30 seconds. The MSK consumer lag alarm I implemented --- which
would have fired within minutes of the Apr~17 backlog beginning to build
--- is the clearest demonstration that this mandate was met.

\subsection{RPA Platforms and Key Business Processes}

The IA platform's automation workloads operate on two RPA technologies:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blue Prism} handles daily, monthly, and quarterly
          fund operations automation. Blue Prism is deployed in pools of
          licensed robot nodes; each pool handles a designated class of
          work (e.g., DataOps P1--P20 in PROD).

    \item \textbf{UiPath} --- both UiPath Cloud (most processes, post
          migration) and UiPath on-premises (non-cloud, for processes such
          as FX Settled Status audits that run within the Citco VPC) ---
          is the target platform for Blue Prism migrations and hosts newer
          automation processes.
\end{itemize}

The key business processes monitored by the IA-SRE team include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{VPL Pricing} --- Valuation and Pricing (VPL) queue
          monitored from 5~PM EST daily. Three primary bots (with capacity
          for three additional) handle fund NAV and pricing completion
          events under a tight business deadline driven by market close.

    \item \textbf{Corporate Actions} --- Mandatory, Voluntary, and
          Operations Transfer workflows, plus Two Sigma wire transfers.

    \item \textbf{MESO} --- Middle Office Month-End Sign-Off
          (\texttt{ia\_meso\_process}), a Python and AWS-based process
          handling cash and position reconciliation, Aexeo report
          generation, and E-Binder packaging for fund administration teams.

    \item \textbf{CAIS Deadline} --- Consolidated Audit Trail (CAIS)
          submission automation under the \texttt{ia-bpo} budget code,
          subject to regulatory filing deadlines.
\end{itemize}
