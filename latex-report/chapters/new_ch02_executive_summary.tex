\chapter{Executive Summary}
\label{chap:executive_summary}

This report documents my Mitacs Business Strategy Internship (BSI)
at Citco Technology Management (CTM), Halifax, Nova Scotia, conducted between
\textbf{March~15 and August~31, 2026} under the project title
\textit{``Intelligent Automation and Reliability Engineering for Financial
Services''}.

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{The Problem}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

When I joined the IA-SRE team in March~2026, two documented production
failures had already established the cost of operating without centralized
observability.

\subsection*{IISS-430 --- CAIS-Pricing GenAI Pipeline: 29-Day Silent Outage (Jan--Feb 2026)}

Between \textbf{January~20 and February~17, 2026} --- roughly five weeks before my
internship began --- the CAIS-Pricing Extract GenAI pipeline under the
\texttt{rpacfs1} platform stopped delivering output entirely.
Three downstream Lambda functions dropped to
\textbf{zero invocations} while the two upstream functions kept running at
millions of calls per day, making the pipeline appear healthy to a
casual observer. The root cause was gradual exhaustion of the CTI AI
model API token, which controls the async SQS callback that triggers
the downstream stages. Without that callback, the downstream Lambda
functions were simply never invoked.

Zero pricing data files (CSVs) were delivered across January Weeks~3--4
and February Weeks~1--3. The outage was eventually detected not by any
monitoring system, but by \textbf{business users reporting missing
data in their reports} --- 29~days after it began.

Sub-task IISS-435 --- \textit{Create Unified CloudWatch Dashboard} ---
was assigned directly to me as the immediate remediation for the gaps
identified in incident IISS-430, and became the starting point for the
full monitoring programme.

\subsection*{IISS-472 --- Meridian Event Processing Delay: April~17, 2026}

The second failure occurred during my first week of training.
On \textbf{April~17, 2026}, a high volume of automated system events
from Citco Recs congested the OpenSearch consumption layer on the
Meridian platform. Events were successfully received in Databricks but
were not being consumed into OpenSearch, causing Kafka consumer lag to
reach approximately \textbf{9~million messages}. Because AXI polls
Meridian for completion events, the stalled consumer meant AXI saw no
completions and daily statement processing halted for multiple fund
clients --- including Winton and Goldman Sachs dailies --- for
\textbf{7.5~hours}.

The SRE team received no alert. Detection came only when the Dublin
business team escalated at 08:13~AST, more than three and a half hours
after the first failure signal. The official incident post-mortem
recorded explicitly:

\begin{quote}
\textit{``No proactive monitoring/alerting in place on the SRE side for
Meridian event volume anomalies. Reliance on business teams to flag
processing delays.''}
\end{quote}

Action items 1 and 2 from that post-mortem --- assigned to Kri Labos
--- became the core functional requirements for IISS-487, the centralized
dashboard I subsequently built as a Proof-of-Concept (PoC).

\subsection*{The Shared Root Cause}

Both incidents share the same structural failure: the IA-SRE team had
no centralized observability platform. Across Lambda functions,
ECS clusters, EC2 instances, and critical Kafka-backed pipelines
spanning six platforms --- \texttt{rpacfs1}, \texttt{ia-bpo},
\texttt{automationhub}, \texttt{meridian}, \texttt{citcoworks}, and
\texttt{bpm} (internal budget-code identifiers used for cost allocation
and AWS service tagging across the \textbf{IA-IT-SRE Infrastructure}) --- the primary
incident detection mechanism was \textbf{business-team escalation}.

Citco also uses \textbf{Dynatrace} for BPM automation observability,
but its enterprise licensing cost scales with infrastructure size.
My industry supervisor, Kishor Deotale, framed the research question
for the Mitacs engagement: \textit{can AWS-native services achieve
Dynatrace-equivalent observability at a fraction of the cost?}

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{The Approach}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

My approach was incremental --- each phase addressed the most
pressing gap, and the output of each phase fed the next.

\textbf{Phase 1: CloudWatch Dashboards for Lambda (IISS-435, IISS-455, IISS-461, IISS-469).}
The immediate remediation for IISS-430 was a unified CloudWatch dashboard
for the \texttt{rpacfs1} and \texttt{ia-bpo} Lambda fleets, later expanded
across all six platforms. I built this using Infrastructure-as-Code (IaC)
CloudFormation templates on CloudWatch and extended it to cover additional
platforms (BPM, CitcoWorks, Meridian) in IISS-469. Lambda was the natural
starting point because it was the AWS service at the centre of the CAIS silent
outage, and because AWS exposes rich native metrics --- invocation count, error
rate, duration, throttles, and concurrent executions --- without any agent
installation. Lambda trigger types --- SQS-based, scheduled (EventBridge), and
API-Gateway-based --- were documented alongside each function in the
Confluence inventory (IISS-461) to make operational intent visible.

\textbf{Phase 2: IA Infrastructure Monitoring --- Dynamic AWS Service Inventory (IISS-482--486, IISS-507--510).}
Static dashboards break as fleets grow. To make AWS service discovery dynamic,
I built a registry-based inventory that queries the AWS API at runtime and
returns structured lists of deployed AWS services per platform. The inventory
evolved through three iterations: an initial FastAPI prototype, a conversion
to Flask to align with the team's existing Lambda toolchain using the
\texttt{serverless-wsgi} adapter, and finally a Lambda-deployed async Flask
API (\texttt{ia-it-sre-infrastructure-inventory}, IISS-507). The inventory
covers Lambda functions, ECS clusters (with trigger type metadata), EC2
instances, SQS queues, and S3 buckets across all six platforms:
\texttt{rpacfs1}, \texttt{ia-bpo}, \texttt{automationhub}, \texttt{meridian},
\texttt{citcoworks}, and \texttt{bpm}. A critical discovery during this phase
was that the \texttt{meridian} and \texttt{citcoworks} platforms share the
same internal \texttt{BudgetCode} tag value (\texttt{"Shared Cloud
Artifacts"}), requiring \texttt{AppName} as a mandatory secondary
discriminator for AWS service discovery in those platforms.

\textbf{Phase 3: Grafana AMG Centralized Dashboard (IISS-487).}
While the CloudWatch dashboards from Phase~1 gave per-platform visibility
into individual AWS services, the Grafana PoC answered a different question:
\textit{what is the health of the entire IA-IT-SRE Infrastructure, right now,
across all platforms and all AWS services simultaneously?} I built a
14-row Amazon Managed Grafana (AMG) workspace with collapsible panels for
each AWS service and dynamic CloudWatch alarms, starting with Meridian
(the platform with the most documented monitoring gaps) and extending to
\texttt{rpacfs1}, \texttt{ia-bpo}, \texttt{automationhub}, \texttt{bpm}, and
\texttt{citcoworks} via platform template variables. Each row covers one
AWS service across the IA-IT-SRE Infrastructure: Lambda, SQS, MSK/Kafka,
EC2, ECR, EKS, ECS Services, ECS Target Groups, RDS Aurora, DocumentDB,
Redis/Valkey, Neptune DB, and OpenSearch. The IaC provisioner --- a
Lambda-backed CloudFormation pipeline --- dynamically discovers the
current AWS service fleet at deploy time and synthesizes per-service alarm
definitions, enabling a single \texttt{sam deploy} to provision alarms
across all monitored AWS services without manual enumeration.

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Limitations and Mitigations}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Several CloudWatch and AMG constraints shaped the final architecture.

\textbf{CloudWatch \texttt{SEARCH()} visibility gap.} The \texttt{SEARCH()}
function only returns metrics for AWS services with recent activity. Lambda
functions not invoked within the lookback window disappear from the
dashboard entirely --- the same root cause that made the CAIS pipeline
invisible in IISS-430. \textit{Mitigation:} Replaced all
\texttt{SEARCH()} expressions with CloudWatch Metric Insights SQL
(\texttt{SELECT ... GROUP BY FunctionName}), which enumerates all
deployed AWS services in the namespace regardless of recent activity.

\textbf{Metric Insights SQL cannot compute percentiles.} The
\texttt{GROUP BY} mode of Metric Insights only supports \texttt{AVG},
\texttt{SUM}, \texttt{MIN}, and \texttt{MAX}. P50/P99 latency statistics
are unavailable in this mode. \textit{Mitigation:} Latency percentile
panels use CloudWatch standard metrics mode with a
\texttt{FunctionName: ["*"]} wildcard dimension. This restores true
percentiles but reintroduces the visibility gap for idle AWS services ---
an accepted tradeoff documented in the dashboard design.

\textbf{Secrets Manager 64\,KB payload limit.} The Lambda-backed
CloudFormation provisioner stores alarm templates as a Secrets Manager
payload. As the monitoring scope expanded to cover all AWS service
categories, the generated YAML exceeded the hard 64\,KB limit.
\textit{Mitigation:} Migrated all templates to an S3 bucket with object
versioning; the provisioner fetches templates via \texttt{s3:GetObject}
at deploy time.

\textbf{Shared \texttt{BudgetCode} tag across platforms.} The
\texttt{meridian} and \texttt{citcoworks} platforms share the internal
tag value \texttt{"Shared Cloud Artifacts"}, making a single-stage
tag lookup ambiguous when discovering AWS services in those platforms.
\textit{Mitigation:} Designated \texttt{AppName} as the mandatory
secondary discriminator for all AWS service discovery in those platforms,
documented as a platform-wide governance constraint in the
inventory API \texttt{README}.

\section{Key Results}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\begin{itemize}
    \item Kafka consumer lag detection time reduced from a 3.5-hour
          business-team escalation window to \textbf{under 30 seconds}
          via a CloudWatch MSK \texttt{EstimatedMaxTimeLag} alarm ---
          directly addressing the April~17 Meridian incident failure mode
    \item Dynamic CloudWatch alarms deployed across all monitored AWS
          services on all six platforms (was zero alarms before)
    \item Grafana AMG workspace with \textbf{14 rows} covering every AWS
          service category in the IA-IT-SRE Infrastructure
    \item Lambda function inventory across all six platforms, catalogued
          with trigger type (SQS-based, EventBridge-scheduled, API-Gateway),
          runtime, and metric-emission status
    \item AWS service inventory extended beyond Lambda to cover ECS, EC2,
          SQS, EKS, ECR, and database services via the inventory API,
          with \textbf{99\% automated test coverage}
    \item AWS-native stack achieves \textbf{$\sim$60\% Dynatrace feature
          parity} at \$77/month (CloudWatch + Grafana AMG + X-Ray),
          rising to \textbf{$\sim$80\%} with Application Signals ---
          versus Dynatrace enterprise licensing cost
\end{itemize}

The three capabilities that remain outside AWS-native reach are Davis~AI
causal analysis, automatic anomaly baselining, and session replay ---
approximately 20\% of Dynatrace's functional surface with no direct
AWS equivalent.

Despite these limitations, the PoC demonstrates that AWS-native tooling
can close the most operationally critical gaps --- proactive alerting,
cross-service visibility, and AWS service inventory --- at a fraction of
the enterprise APM cost. The chapters that follow detail the
methodologies, architecture, and engineering decisions behind each
deliverable, and reflect on the broader learning outcomes of the
engagement.
